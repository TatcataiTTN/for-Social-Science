PART 2/5
02

Probability sampling

  • Simple random & systematic
  • Stratified sampling
  • Cluster & multi-stage sampling
2 · Simple random & systematic

Every unit has an EQUAL chance of selection

Simple random: draw at random from the whole frame.

Systematic: select every k-th unit (e.g. every 10th student on the list).

Stratified

Split into groups first, then sample randomly WITHIN each

Split the frame by a relevant characteristic (region, school type), ensuring each stratum appears in the sample in proportion.

PISA does exactly this — stratifying by region before selecting schools.

Cluster & multi-stage

Select whole CLUSTERS instead of individuals

Randomly select schools (clusters), then measure ALL or a group of students inside — much cheaper than randomly selecting individual students nationwide.

PISA: stage 1 selects schools (clusters), stage 2 selects students within — "multi-stage".

PART 3/5
03

Non-probability sampling

  • Convenience, purposive, quota, snowball
  • When it's acceptable
3 · Four common types

The selection probability of each unit is unknown

Convenience: whoever is easiest to reach. Purposive: selected against a deliberate criterion.

Quota: fill a target number per group, not random within it. Snowball: one participant refers the next.

When it's acceptable

Not representative — but not always wrong to use

Suitable for exploratory research, hard-to-reach populations, or when the goal is depth rather than broad generalisation.

The common mistake: using non-probability sampling, then still statistically generalising to the whole population.

1. Population, sampling frame & sample

Three concepts often confused, but must be kept distinct before sampling: the population is the entire group you want to generalise to, in theory; the sampling frame is the ACTUAL list you can draw a sample from; the sample is the subset actually measured.

Coverage error: the gap between the sampling frame and the true population. Example: PISA's frame only includes schools that REGISTERED for the survey — if remote-area schools register less often, the sample can be skewed even though sampling within the frame is perfectly random and correctly executed.

2. Probability sampling

Defining feature: the selection probability of EVERY unit must be determinable (not necessarily equal). This is a required condition for valid statistical inference to the whole population.

TypeHow it worksAdvantageDrawback
Simple randomRandomise the entire frameSimple, unbiased if the frame is goodNeeds a complete frame; costly if geographically scattered
SystematicSelect every k-th unit on the listEasy to execute, spreads evenly across the listRisky if the list has a hidden cycle matching k
StratifiedSplit the frame by a relevant characteristic, sample randomly within eachEnsures every important group appears proportionally, reduces errorRequires knowing the characteristic beforehand
Cluster / multi-stageSelect whole clusters (e.g. schools), then sample withinFar cheaper logisticallyLess precise than simple random at the same sample size
PISA uses both at once: stratifying by 5 regions, then cluster-sampling (schools) within each region with probability proportional to school size — exactly "stratified multi-stage cluster sampling".

3. Non-probability sampling

Defining feature: each unit's selection probability CANNOT be determined. This doesn't mean it's "wrong" — it just means it shouldn't be used for precise statistical generalisation to the whole population.

TypeHow it worksWhen appropriate
ConvenienceTake whoever is easiest to reachPreliminary exploratory research, heavy time/budget constraints
PurposiveSelected deliberately against criteria tied to the research questionNeed experts/typical cases, in-depth qualitative research
QuotaFill target numbers per group, not random within groupNeed proportional groups but lack a complete frame
SnowballParticipants refer the next oneRare, hidden, hard-to-reach populations
Common mistake: using a convenience sample (e.g. surveying the exact class you teach) then still writing conclusions as if applicable to "students in general". Always state the generalisability limitation clearly when using non-probability sampling.

4. Sampling error & evidence-based sample size

Sampling error is the natural difference between sample and population due to chance — it exists even with perfect sampling. Different from sampling bias, which reflects a systematic flaw in the selection process.

Pilot sample size (Julious, 2005, Pharmaceutical Statistics): at least 12 observations/group — below this, the standard error of the estimated SD swings too much (beyond ±20% of the true value) to reliably feed the formal sample-size formula below.

Approximate sample-size formula per group (Cohen, 1988):

Sample size per group — independent t-test
n ≈ (zα/2 + zβ)² × 2 / d²
  • nrequired sample size per group
  • zα/2critical value for significance level α — α=0.05 → z=1.96
  • zβcritical value for β=1−power — power=0.80 → z=0.84
  • dexpected effect size (from a literature review or pilot)
Effect size dLevelSample/group
0.2Small≈394
0.5Medium≈64
0.8Large≈26

5. Applying it to real data: PISA sample weights

Because PISA samples clusters with probability PROPORTIONAL TO school size, schools don't carry equal "weight" during analysis — a large school represents more students in the true population than a small one. The W_NRASCHBWT variable corrects for this.

🖱️ Verified in real SPSS/PSPP — how weighting changes the result:
  1. Open vnm_truong_195_tong_hop.sav.
  2. Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Descriptives... → send EDULEAD in → OK (no weight yet).
  3. Data → Weight Cases... → choose Weight cases by → send W_NRASCHBWT in → OK.
  4. Rerun step 2 — compare the two results.
Real result (verified with PSPP): unweighted mean EDULEAD = 0.7653 (N=195 schools, each counted once). Weighted mean = 0.7874 (effective N=7,284.6, reflecting true student counts). Weights range from 1.0 to 617.2 — meaning the highest-weighted school represents 617 TIMES as many students as the lowest. Ignoring weight here barely changes the conclusion (0.02 difference), but for variables more strongly correlated with school size, the error could be much larger.
WEIGHT BY W_NRASCHBWT.
DESCRIPTIVES VARIABLES=EDULEAD.
* PSPP fully supports the WEIGHT BY command, verified to match hand-calculation in Python.

📜 Theoretical background & history

Modern sampling theory traces back to Jerzy Neyman's 1934 work, the first to rigorously show why PROBABILITY sampling (not the "representative sampling" done by judgement, the common practice before) allows sampling error to be computed objectively — laying the foundation for modern inferential statistics. William G. Cochran's textbook Sampling Techniques (1953) systematised the stratified and cluster techniques still used unchanged today, including in PISA's design.

Timeline
  • 1895 — Anders Kiaer (Norway) proposes the "representative method", an early precursor of sampling.
  • 1934 — Jerzy Neyman proves the mathematical basis for probability sampling and optimal stratification.
  • 1953 — William Cochran publishes Sampling Techniques, systematising cluster/multi-stage theory.
  • 2000–present — the OECD applies a stratified + cluster + multi-stage design to PISA across 80+ countries.

🔎 Real case study

Why doesn't PISA use simple random sampling across all students?

With millions of 15-year-olds scattered across a country, simple random sampling would select students spread across thousands of different schools — survey teams would have to travel to EACH school just to measure 1-2 students, an unfeasible logistical cost. Cluster sampling (by school) trades off some statistical precision for real-world feasibility — this is why PISA's sample (7,368 students) is much larger than the theoretical sample needed under perfect simple random sampling.

Sample weights — why they can't be skipped when analysing real data

In the example above, the gap between the weighted and unweighted mean was only 0.02 — small, because EDULEAD doesn't correlate strongly with school size. But for variables STRONGLY correlated with school size (e.g. facilities, teacher count), ignoring weights could meaningfully bias conclusions — this is why every official OECD PISA report REQUIRES applying weights before publishing any figure.
