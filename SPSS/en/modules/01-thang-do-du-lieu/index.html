1. The four scales of data

Before analysing any number, you must know which scale it belongs to — this determines which operations are legitimate. Four scales, each one subsuming the previous and adding one new feature:

ScaleNew feature addedExampleOperations allowed
NominalClassification onlySex, school type, province codeFrequency counts, mode
Ordinal+ Rank orderLikert scales, academic ranking+ Median, percentiles
Interval+ Equal spacing, NO true zeroTemperature °C/°F, IQ score+ Mean, SD (no ratios)
Ratio+ True zero pointStudy hours, test score (0–10), bank balance+ Every operation, including ratios (“twice as much”)
Classic example (Cohen et al., p.726): you cannot say “100°F is twice as hot as 50°F”, because 0°F does not mean “no heat at all” — it is an interval scale. Twice 50°F is actually 68°F: ((50−32)×2)+32. Likewise, an IQ of 150 is NOT “twice as intelligent” as an IQ of 75, because IQ scales typically start at 70, not at zero.
Key trap in SPSS/PSPP: the software merges Interval and Ratio into a single measurement type called “Scale” — because in practice most statistics treat them the same way (Cohen et al., p.726). In Variable View you only pick one of three: Nominal / Ordinal / Scale.

2. Parametric and non-parametric data

Non-parametric data make no assumptions about the population, usually because its parameters are unknown. Parametric data assume knowledge of population characteristics (usually a normal distribution) so that inferences can be made securely (Cohen et al., p.727).

Rule of thumb: nominal and ordinal → usually treated as non-parametric; interval and ratio → usually treated as parametric (unless badly skewed). Applying parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA…) to non-parametric data is usually wrong; conversely, using non-parametric tests on skewed parametric data is allowed — just less powerful.

3. Descriptive vs. inferential statistics

Descriptive

Simply presents and summarises existing data — no attempt to infer or predict anything about the population.

Includes: mode, mean, median, min/max, range, variance (average of squared deviations from the mean), standard deviation (square root of variance), standard error (SD of sample means), skewness, kurtosis.

Inferential

Attempts to infer/predict population parameters from a sample, based on probability.

Includes: hypothesis testing, regression, difference tests (t-test, ANOVA), factor analysis, structural equation modelling.

4. Independent & dependent variables — why “correlation” is easily misread as “causation”

Independent variable: a variable the researcher can control/manipulate, treated as a potential “cause”. Dependent variable: a variable whose value depends (in part) on the independent variable, treated as the “outcome”.

Cohen et al. (pp.727–728) give this example: more homework hours (independent) → higher maths scores (dependent). But the authors list at least 6 alternative explanations that make “homework caused the higher score” doubtful:

  1. The pressure of the test itself increased in-class concentration (another extraneous variable at work).
  2. Students liked maths/the teacher → worked harder on their own — that liking increased both homework AND scores.
  3. Students slept well the night before → more alert (an unmeasured intervening variable).
  4. Anticipated scores actually drove how much homework students chose to do (reversed causal direction).
  5. The relationship is multi-directional, more like a web than a single causal line.
  6. The threat of punishment for poor performance acted simultaneously.
Lesson for the whole course: most data you will analyse in SPSS/PSPP is observational (not a randomised experiment) — a statistically significant r or β does NOT automatically prove causation.
