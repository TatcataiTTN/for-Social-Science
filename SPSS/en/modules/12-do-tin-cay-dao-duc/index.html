1. Reliability — is the measuring instrument CONSISTENT?

Reliability answers: if you measure the SAME subject again (or use equivalent items), are the results STABLE/CONSISTENT? This is a question about the stability of the MEASUREMENT itself, and says nothing yet about whether that measurement actually captures the intended concept (that's validity, section 2).

Cronbach's Alpha
α = (k / (k−1)) × (1 − Σσᵢ² / σ²ₜ)
  • knumber of items in the scale
  • Σσᵢ²sum of the variances of EACH individual item
  • σ²ₜvariance of the TOTAL SCORE (sum of all items) across the whole sample
  • αranges from 0 to 1 (can be negative in unusual cases) — high α means the items "move together" tightly, measuring one concept consistently
Real data — 2 scales in the classroom dataset (n=237-240):
ScaleNumber of itemsCronbach's αInterpretation level
Interest (h1, h2, h3)30.713Acceptable (0.70-0.80)
Anxiety (a1, a2, a3)30.759Acceptable, close to good
Common interpretation thresholds (not an absolute rule): α<0.60 poor, 0.60-0.70 questionable, 0.70-0.80 acceptable, 0.80-0.90 good, >0.90 excellent (but this can also be a sign of items that are TOO REDUNDANT, see the warning below).
Item-total correlation & Alpha-if-deleted (Interest scale, verified to match PSPP):
ItemItem-total correlationAlpha if this item is deleted
h10.5700.575
h20.4920.672
h30.5350.620
Interpretation: if h2 were DELETED from the scale, alpha would RISE to 0.672 — but that is still LOWER than the current alpha with all 3 items (0.713). Conclusion: keeping all 3 items is the best choice; none should be dropped.
A VERY HIGH alpha (>0.95) isn't always good news: it can be a sign that the items are asking nearly the SAME question in different wording (redundancy) — the scale has "excess" items that add no new information. A good scale balances CONSISTENCY (sufficiently high alpha) with adequately COVERING the concept being measured (not just asking the same thing repeatedly).

2. Validity — does the instrument measure the RIGHT thing?

There is no SINGLE "validity" — it is a collection of different types of evidence, each answering its own question:

Type of validityQuestion it answersExample from this course
Content validityDo the items FULLY cover the concept being measured, or is an important aspect missing?Do the 3 items h1-h3 adequately cover the concept "interest in learning", or do they only tap a narrow slice?
Construct validityDoes the scale really measure the intended THEORETICAL CONCEPT behind it?The EFA in Module 10 "rediscovers" exactly the 2 latent concepts — evidence SUPPORTING construct validity
Criterion validityDoes the score correlate with another trustworthy external criterion?Does the "interest" score correlate with real Maths scores (already seen in Module 09: β=0.229, significant)
Face validityAt a glance, do the items "look like" they measure the right concept? (subjective judgement, the weakest form)The item "Do you enjoy this class?" looks reasonable for measuring interest
EFA (Module 10) as evidence for construct validity: the fact that EFA "rediscovers" exactly 2 factors (interest, anxiety) matching the original theoretical design is a form of evidence REINFORCING construct validity — if EFA had produced a completely different structure than expected (e.g. all 6 items collapsing into a single factor, or splitting into 4 scattered factors), that would be a WARNING sign about the scale's construct validity.

3. Reliability does NOT guarantee validity — and vice versa

The classic example: a faulty scale ALWAYS reads 2kg heavier than the true weight. This scale has very high RELIABILITY (measuring again and again always gives a consistent result, off by exactly 2kg each time) — but very LOW VALIDITY (it doesn't measure true weight). Reliability is a NECESSARY but NOT SUFFICIENT condition for validity — an unreliable scale (results scattered inconsistently across measurements) certainly cannot have high validity, but high reliability doesn't automatically guarantee high validity.

Applied to the classroom dataset: α=0.713 (interest) tells us the 3 items h1-h3 measure CONSISTENTLY with each other — but this does NOT automatically prove that these 3 items really measure the concept "interest in learning" in its true theoretical sense (they might inadvertently measure a related but different concept, such as "compliance" or "wanting to please the teacher"). Additional validity evidence (content, construct, criterion) is needed to establish that.

4. Research ethics

Three core ethical principles that must be considered in EVERY study collecting data from human subjects, especially important for the PISA data (15-year-old students — minors) used throughout this course:

PrincipleMeaningApplied to the PISA context
Informed consentParticipants (or their guardians, for minors) understand the purpose, procedure, and right to refuse BEFORE agreeing to take partPISA needs consent from both the school AND the parents/students before data collection
Confidentiality & anonymityPersonal data is protected, and cannot be traced back to a specific participant's identityThe student ID (CNTSTUID) and school ID (CNTSCHID) are anonymised codes, containing no real names
Avoidance of harmThe research must not cause psychological/social harm to participants, even indirectly (e.g. publishing data that lets a school/students be identified and stigmatised)The public dataset does not disclose specific school names, only ID codes
Connecting to the whole course: every statistical technique learned so far (Modules 01-11) assumes the input data was collected ETHICALLY — no statistical method can "fix" an ethics problem at the data-collection stage. A technically perfect regression model (Module 09) is still completely WORTHLESS if the underlying data was collected without valid consent.

5. SPSS/PSPP practice

🖱️ Step-by-step clicks in SPSS:
  1. File → Open → Data... → select lop_hoc_240.sav.
  2. Analyze → Scale → Reliability Analysis...
  3. Move h1, h2, h3 into the Items box.
  4. Click Statistics... → check Scale if item deleted (under Descriptives for) → Continue → OK.
  5. Read the "Reliability Statistics" table (Cronbach's Alpha) and "Item-Total Statistics" (item-total correlation, alpha if item deleted).

PSPP equivalent (verified to run 100% consistently — see pspp/12_do_tin_cay_en.sps):

GET FILE='lop_hoc_240.sav'.
RELIABILITY
  /VARIABLES=h1 h2 h3
  /SCALE('Interest') ALL
  /MODEL=ALPHA
  /SUMMARY=TOTAL.
* Verified result: Cronbach's Alpha=.71, matches the hand
* calculation and Python exactly.
