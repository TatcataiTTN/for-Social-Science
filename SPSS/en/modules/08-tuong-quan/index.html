2. Pearson r: direction, strength, and meaning

The coefficient r runs from −1 to +1. The sign (+/−) indicates direction: moving together (positive) or in opposite directions (negative). The magnitude |r| indicates strength — r itself already IS the effect size, no separate calculation needed (p.767).

The classic trap — correlation is not causation (p.768): "big hands correlate with big feet" does not mean big hands cause big feet. Both are more likely linked to a third variable (overall body size). Always ask: is there a third variable explaining both?

A real-data example (classroom data, n=240)

How to report it, following the book's style (p.768): "Using Pearson's correlation, a large effect size (r = 0.58) and a statistically significant correlation (p < 0.001) were found between study hours and maths score. Students who studied more tended to score higher." — but not "studying more caused higher scores" (this is observational data).

3. When the relationship is NOT a straight line

Pearson r assumes a linear relationship. Cohen et al.'s example (p.769): moderate stress improves focus (positive correlation), excessive stress reduces performance (negative correlation) — an inverted-U shape, not a straight line. Computing Pearson r across the whole range can come out near zero despite a very clear relationship, because the positive and negative parts cancel out in a single coefficient. Always plot the scatterplot first before trusting the r value.

4. Partial correlation

Used when a third variable is suspected of "mixing into" the relationship between two main variables. The book's example (p.770): the correlation between basketball experience and performance may be confounded by height — partial correlation removes height's influence to reveal the "true" relationship between the two main variables.

Trying it on real PISA Vietnam data (195 schools)

The zero-order correlation between material shortage (EDUSHORT) and the school's average science score: r = −0.039 (very weak). Suspected confound: staff shortage (STAFFSHORT), which correlates fairly highly with material shortage (r=0.61) — analogous to "height" in the basketball example. After controlling for staff shortage, the partial correlation is: r = −0.051 — almost unchanged.

Why does this differ from the book's example? A partial correlation shifts substantially only when the control variable has a REAL relationship with both other variables. Here, staff shortage is almost unrelated to the science score (r≈0.002) — so "removing" it barely changes the original picture. This is a genuine result, not every dataset produces as dramatic an example as the textbook's.

5. SPSS/PSPP practice

🖱️ Step-by-step mouse clicks in SPSS:

0. Clean the outlier FIRST (mandatory — skip this and r comes out ≈0.304, not the 0.582 shown in this lesson):

  1. Transform → Compute Variable...
  2. Target Variable box: type study_hours (overwriting this same variable).
  3. Numeric Expression box: type study_hours / 10.
  4. Click If... → choose Include if case satisfies condition → type the condition study_hours > 20 → Continue.
  5. Click OK → confirm overwriting the variable. (One student has study_hours=48, almost certainly a typo for "4.8" — see Module 01/04.)

A. Pearson correlation (study hours × maths score):

  1. File → Open → Data... → select lop_hoc_240.sav → Open.
  2. Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate...
  3. Select study_hours then math, click the arrow to send both to the Variables box.
  4. Under "Correlation Coefficients", make sure Pearson is checked (default); under "Test of Significance" choose Two-tailed.
  5. Click OK.
  6. Read the "Correlations" table (a 2×2 matrix): the cell where study_hours and math meet has 3 rows — Pearson Correlation (the r value), Sig. (2-tailed) (the p-value), N (the sample size used).

B. Spearman correlation (when data are ordinal/non-linear):

  1. Repeat steps 1-3 from part A, but under "Correlation Coefficients" ALSO check Spearman (you can uncheck Pearson if you only need Spearman).
  2. Click OK → the results table will have 2 separate blocks: "Pearson Correlation" and "Spearman's rho" for comparison.

C. Partial correlation — controlling for a third variable (⚠️ real SPSS only — PSPP reports "PARTIAL CORR is not yet implemented", verified):

  1. Analyze → Correlate → Partial...
  2. Send the 2 variables you want to correlate (e.g. EDUSHORT and sci_mean) to the Variables box.
  3. Send the variable you want to CONTROL FOR (e.g. STAFFSHORT) to the Controlling for box.
  4. Click Options... → check Zero-order correlations (so SPSS also prints the ORIGINAL uncontrolled correlation for comparison) → Continue.
  5. Click OK → read the results table: the first row is the partial correlation (controlled); the "Zero-order" section (if checked in step 4) gives the original correlation, letting you see how much controlling for the third variable changed things.

📜 Theoretical & Historical Background

The concept of "correlation" was born from a very concrete question: in 1888, Francis Galton (Charles Darwin's cousin) studied how closely children's height resembled their parents'. He called this phenomenon "co-relation". Galton's striking discovery: children of very tall parents tended to be slightly shorter than their parents (closer to the population average) — a phenomenon he called "regression toward the mean", laying the foundation for BOTH correlation AND regression (Module 09) at once.

Eight years later, in 1896, statistician Karl Pearson — Galton's student — formalised the "co-relation" idea into the precise mathematical formula you use in SPSS today: the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, the origin of the name "Pearson r". In 1904, psychologist Charles Spearman developed a version for ranked data (not requiring a normal-distribution assumption) — becoming the Spearman's rho you learn in this module.

Timeline relevant to this module
  • 1888 — Francis Galton studies height heredity, coins "co-relation", and discovers "regression toward the mean".
  • 1896 — Karl Pearson formalises the product-moment correlation formula — "Pearson's r" is born.
  • 1904 — Charles Spearman introduces the rank correlation coefficient — "Spearman's rho".
  • Today — Tyler Vigen's tylervigen.com/spurious-correlations catalogues hundreds of pairs of variables with high, entirely coincidental correlations, widely used in teaching to illustrate "correlation ≠ causation".

🔎 Real-World Case Studies

Case 1 — Galton's original height data

Galton measured the heights of hundreds of parent-child pairs. He plotted a scatterplot (the exact chart type you learned in Module 03 and reuse here), and noticed the points formed a tilted elliptical cloud — not a perfect straight line, but a clear tendency. From the shape of that cloud, he and later Pearson devised a way to SUMMARISE that tendency with a SINGLE number — exactly the core idea the r coefficient still does today.

Case 2 — "Spurious correlation": Nicolas Cage films and drownings

Tyler Vigen's famous "Spurious Correlations" project once showed that the number of Nicolas Cage films released per year correlates VERY highly (r above 0.6, nearing 0.9 in some years) with the number of people who drowned in swimming pools in the US that same year — despite obviously no causal link. This is an extreme (and deliberately humorous) illustration of exactly the "correlation is not causation" lesson (p.768) this module emphasises: with enough variables and enough years of data, high coincidental correlations are statistically unremarkable (connecting to the p-hacking lesson in Module 06/07 of the original course).
