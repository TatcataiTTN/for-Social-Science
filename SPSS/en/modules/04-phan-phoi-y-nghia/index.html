1. Hypotheses: H0, H1, directional & one-/two-tailed

Null hypothesis (H0): states there is "no" difference/relationship/change. Cohen et al. compare this to a jury: presume innocence (H0 true) until there's enough evidence against it. Alternative hypothesis (H1): states "there is" — only supported once H0 is not supported (p.729).

Choose wording carefully: the book recommends "supported / not supported", avoiding "reject/accept" — the latter imply an absolute state that a bounded study can rarely establish (pp.729–730).

Directional vs non-directional: a directional hypothesis states the direction ("higher", "more"); non-directional just says "different". A directional hypothesis is stronger because it makes a more specific claim (pp.731–732).

Hypothesis typeTest typeWhy
DirectionalOne-tailedAll 5% risk placed on the predicted side
Non-directionalTwo-tailedRisk split 2.5%/side — needs stronger evidence (97.5%)

2. Confidence Intervals

The APA (2010, p.34, cited p.733) REQUIRES confidence intervals in published findings. A CI tells you: if the sampling procedure were repeated many times, what % of the constructed intervals would contain the TRUE population value (typically 95%).

Real-data example: mean maths score (n=240) = 47.91, SE = 0.625 → 95% CI = [46.68; 49.14]. Correct interpretation: "if sampling 240 students were repeated many times, ~95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true population mean" — not "there's a 95% probability the true mean falls in this interval" (p.733).

Torgerson & Torgerson (2008, p.135, cited p.734): small samples → wide CIs (less precise); large samples → narrower CIs (more precise).

3. The normal distribution, skewness & kurtosis

Positive skew: a long tail toward the POSITIVE (right) end. Negative skew: a long tail toward the NEGATIVE (left) end. Platykurtic: flatter than normal (negative kurtosis). Leptokurtic: more peaked than normal (positive kurtosis) (pp.734–735).

Rule of thumb for 'meaningful' skew (pp.735–736): compare skewness to ±2×SE(skewness). For study_hours: skew=+0.658, SE=0.157 → acceptable range [-0.314; +0.314]. 0.658 EXCEEDS this range → meaningful skew — matching Shapiro-Wilk W=0.969, p<0.001 (H0 of 'normal data' not supported). By contrast pretest (skew=-0.097) falls within range, Shapiro-Wilk p=0.117 (insufficient evidence against normality).

4. What is statistical significance?

Kirk (1999, p.337, cited p.739): "a statistically significant result is one for which chance is an unlikely explanation." Cohen et al.'s classic example (pp.739–740): observing "big hands go with big feet" in 95/100 people → the 0.05 significance level; in 99/100 → the 0.01 level; in 999/1,000 → the 0.001 level.

Worked example (pp.740–741): with 8 people, a measured rank correlation of 0.7857. Looking up the significance table: n=8 needs r≥0.78 (0.05 level) and r≥0.875 (0.01 level). 0.7857 exceeds 0.78 but not 0.875 → significant at the 0.05 level, not at 0.01. With n=30, only r≥0.36 is needed for significance at 0.05 — the larger the sample, the smaller the required r threshold.
Important warning: "statistically significant" does NOT mean "important" — a common confusion among novice researchers (p.740).

5. Critiquing NHST — why some journals no longer accept papers relying only on p-values

Some journals have stopped accepting papers relying solely on null hypothesis significance testing (NHST). Carver (1978, p.387, cited p.742) calls it a "corrupt scientific method". Three main reasons:

  1. H0 is a "straw man": in reality H0 is almost never absolutely true — yet the entire NHST procedure is built on this assumption (Carver, 1978, pp.380–382).
  2. The "transposed conditional" fallacy (Ziliak & McCloskey, 2008, p.41): confusing P(data | H0 true) with P(H0 true | data). Carver's example: the probability a person is DEAD given they were hanged is very high, but the probability a person was hanged given they are DEAD is very low — a p-value only answers the first.
  3. Heavy dependence on sample size: the larger the sample, the easier it is to reach significance even with a tiny coefficient (pp.742–743).
Statistical significance ≠ practical significance: one could find a statistically significant correlation between time spent on maths and time spent shopping — utterly unimportant (p.743). Conversely, p=0.065 (just above 0.05) shouldn't be hastily read as "no difference" — the 0.05 cutoff is a fairly arbitrary convention (p.743).

Cohen et al. do not ask you to abandon NHST entirely, but recommend shifting toward effect size, statistical power, and other verification methods (replication, ruling out alternative hypotheses) (p.744).

6. Effect Size

Effect size measures the MAGNITUDE of a difference/relationship — something a p-value alone doesn't tell you. Cohen et al. note "effect size" is an inaccurate term: "effect" implies causation, but this measure proves no causation whatsoever (p.745).

Applied to real classroom data:
  • Teaching method (Project/Traditional) → score gain: Cohen's d = 0.981 → moderate (0.51–1.00), close to the large threshold.
  • 3 schools → maths score: eta² = SSB/SST = 1056.82/22374.01 = 0.047 → small, despite a statistically significant F test (p=0.003)!
This is a live demonstration: statistical significance and effect size are two different questions that can give counter-intuitive answers.

7. Statistical power

Power = the probability of correctly detecting a real effect (1−β). Power=0.80 (a common benchmark) means an 80% chance of finding a real effect, 20% risk of missing it (Type II). Cohen (1988, p.750) proposes treating Type I as 4 times more serious than Type II at α=0.05, β=0.20.

Four parameters determine power: (1) effect size, (2) sample size, (3) α level, (4) desired β level (p.751). The book's rough estimates (α=0.05, power=0.80): a two-tailed difference test seeking d=0.5 needs ~130 people; a two-tailed correlation test seeking r=0.5 needs only ~30.

📜 Theoretical & Historical Background

NHST is a blend of two historically incompatible traditions: Ronald Fisher (1920s) developed significance testing and popularised the p=0.05 threshold, while Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson (Karl Pearson's son, late 1920s–1930s) developed the "hypothesis testing" framework with two competing hypotheses (H0/H1) and the concept of Type I/Type II error. Today's SPSS blends both traditions into a single procedure — exactly the source of the conceptual confusion the book flags on p.742 (Gigerenzer, 2004, calls this an "unplanned hybrid tool" no one deliberately designed).

Timeline
  • 1920s — Ronald Fisher develops significance testing, popularising the p=0.05 threshold.
  • Late 1920s–1930s — Neyman & Pearson develop the H0/H1 framework and Type I/II errors.
  • 1978 — Carver publishes a sharp critique of NHST, calling it a "corrupt scientific method".
  • 1988 — Jacob Cohen publishes Statistical Power Analysis, introducing the effect-size thresholds still used today.
  • 2010 — APA requires confidence intervals to be reported in published research.

🔎 Real-World Case Studies

Case 1 — The psychology "Replication Crisis"

In the early 2010s, the Reproducibility Project (Open Science Collaboration, 2015) attempted to replicate 100 published psychology studies (most relying on NHST with p<0.05). Fewer than 40% replicated the original statistically significant result. This is the largest real-world demonstration of the critique on pp.742–743: a single p-value, from a single sample, is not reliable enough to build an entire scientific finding on — exactly the "dancing p-values" lesson you'll revisit later in this course.

Case 2 — When a huge sample makes everything "significant"

With administrative/web-scale data of millions of rows, almost EVERY comparison yields p<0.001 — including practically meaningless differences (like eta²=0.047 in the three-school example above). This is exactly why major journals (British Medical Journal, American Psychologist...) now mandate reporting effect size, not just p-values — the very topic of section 6 above.
