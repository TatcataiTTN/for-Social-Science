1 · Why ANOVA

A t-test only compares 2 groups — what about 3+?

ANOVA extends the t-test: comparing 3+ group means at once, under the same 4 safety checks.

Example: exam scores across 4 school types (rural/urban × primary/secondary).

The F-ratio

Variation BETWEEN groups vs WITHIN groups

F = Between-groups variance / Within-groups variance

A large F → the difference between groups exceeds the random noise within each group.

Real data

Maths score by 3 schools: F(2,237)=5.87, p=0.003

Key point

ANOVA says "there IS a difference" — not "WHERE"

A significant F only tells you: a difference exists somewhere among the groups.

A post-hoc test is needed to pinpoint EXACTLY which pair differs!

1. Why ANOVA & safety checks

A t-test can only compare the means of 2 groups. When educational research needs to compare 3 or more groups (e.g. exam scores across 4 school types), we need Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) — based on the SAME parametric assumptions as the t-test (p.781):

#Condition
1Continuous, parametric data (interval/ratio)
2Random sampling
3Normal distribution (large samples usually compensate)
4Homogeneity of variance (Levene's test; if violated, SPSS offers the more robust Brown-Forsythe & Welch)

ANOVA computes the F-ratio = variance BETWEEN groups divided by variance WITHIN each group. A large F means the difference between groups far exceeds random variation within each group.

2. One-way ANOVA

Cohen et al.'s example (pp.781–782): standardised maths exam scores across 4 school types (rural primary=59.85%; rural secondary=60.44%; urban primary=50.64%; urban secondary=51.70%). H0: "no significant difference among the 4 means". SPSS result: F(3, df)=8.976, p=0.000 → H0 rejected, a significant difference exists somewhere among the 4 groups.

Real classroom data (3 schools A/B/C, maths score): School A (n=80) M=50.67; School B (n=99) M=47.26; School C (n=61) M=45.34. One-way ANOVA: F(2,237)=5.87, p=0.003 → a significant difference exists. Levene's test on the 3 schools' variances: p=0.292 (≥0.05) → variances are homogeneous, satisfying Tukey's condition.
What the F-test does NOT tell you: a significant F only says "a difference exists somewhere among the groups" — NOT that every pair differs, nor which pair (p.782). The next step: post-hoc testing.

3. Tukey post-hoc & homogeneous subsets

A post-hoc test's purpose is to find EXACTLY which pairs of groups differ significantly (p.782). Tukey HSD assumes homogeneous variance and roughly equal sub-sample sizes; if not met, Games-Howell is used instead.

Real Tukey results (maths score, 3 schools):
PairMean diff.p (adj)Conclusion
A – B3.410.046Significant
A – C5.340.0031Significant
B – C1.930.427Not significant
→ Homogeneous subsets: {A} stands apart, {B, C} form a shared group — School A differs clearly from both B and C, while B and C don't differ substantially.

The book's original example (4 school types, pp.783–784) shows a similar pattern: the two rural schools (primary+secondary) group together, the two urban schools group together — conclusion: the difference is due to geographic location (rural/urban), not school level.

Correct write-up (per the p.784 template): "Analysis of Variance found a statistically significant difference between the 3 schools (F(2,237)=5.87, p=0.003). The Tukey test showed that School A (M=50.67) differed significantly from School B (M=47.26, p=0.046) and School C (M=45.34, p=0.003), while Schools B and C did not differ significantly from each other (p=0.427)." Cohen et al. recommend using both tests together: ANOVA answers "is there a difference", Tukey answers "where" (p.783).

4. Two-way ANOVA & interaction effects

Two-way ANOVA estimates the effect of TWO categorical independent variables on one continuous dependent variable, plus an interaction effect: whether variable A's effect differs by level of variable B (pp.784–785).

Real two-way ANOVA results (maths score ~ school × gender):
EffectFdfpConclusion
School (main effect)5.812, 2340.003Significant
Gender (main effect)1.711, 2340.192Not significant
School × Gender (interaction)1.162, 2340.315Not significant
Interpretation: school produces a real difference in maths scores, but this effect does NOT differ meaningfully between males and females (no interaction) — school's effect can be stated fairly generally for both genders.
What if the interaction WERE significant? Then stating the main effect "in general" (e.g. "school always affects scores the same way") would be misleading — a more cautious interpretation is needed, since one variable's effect depends on the level of the other.

5. MANOVA — when there are multiple dependent variables

MANOVA (Multiple Analysis of Variance) extends ANOVA to cases with MULTIPLE continuous dependent variables analysed simultaneously (e.g. several exam subjects at once), instead of just one dependent variable (pp.785–786).

Warning: MANOVA is "very sensitive to the assumptions" (p.786) — multivariate normality and equality of covariance matrices must be carefully checked before trusting the results, since violations here more easily lead to wrong conclusions than in univariate ANOVA.

Read the results in order: "Test of Homogeneity of Variances" table (check Levene's Sig. to decide standard ANOVA vs. Welch) → "ANOVA" table (the Sig. column on the "Between Groups" row is the main p-value) → "Multiple Comparisons" table (the Tukey HSD section, check the Sig. column for each school pair) → "Homogeneous Subsets" table (to see which groups are lumped together).

B. Two-way ANOVA (maths score by school × gender):

  1. Analyze → General Linear Model → Univariate...
  2. Select math, click the arrow to send it to the Dependent Variable box.
  3. Select school and gender, click the arrow to send both to the Fixed Factor(s) box.
  4. Click OK (SPSS automatically adds the school*gender interaction to the default model).
  5. Read the "Tests of Between-Subjects Effects" table: find the 3 rows school, gender, school * gender — each has its own Sig. column; the interaction row tells you whether one variable's effect depends on the other.

📜 Theoretical & Historical Background

Ronald Fisher developed ANOVA in the 1920s while working at the Rothamsted agricultural experiment station (England), where he needed a method to compare crop yields across MANY experimental plots (multiple fertilisers, multiple crop varieties) at once — running many separate pairwise t-tests would have sharply inflated the risk of Type I error. The "F" in F-ratio is named after Fisher (proposed later by George Snedecor in his honour). The Tukey HSD test was introduced by John Tukey in 1953, solving exactly the "many pairwise comparisons" problem without the exploding false-positive risk of running many separate t-tests.

Timeline
  • 1918–1925 — Fisher develops analysis of variance at Rothamsted, applied to agricultural experiments.
  • 1925 — Fisher publishes Statistical Methods for Research Workers, systematising ANOVA.
  • 1953 — John Tukey introduces the "honestly significant difference" (HSD) test.
  • 1980s–1990s — Bonferroni, Scheffé, Games-Howell become more widely used in social science.

🔎 Real Case Study

Case 1 — Why "running multiple t-tests" is a common trap

A study comparing 4 groups (e.g. 4 teaching methods) that runs 6 separate pairwise t-tests, each at α=0.05, has a much higher probability of making AT LEAST one Type I error than running a single ANOVA plus an adjusted post-hoc test (like Tukey). This is exactly the historical reason Fisher invented ANOVA at Rothamsted — solving the multi-group agricultural comparison problem without "fishing" for false positives.

Case 2 — Two-way ANOVA in evaluating an educational intervention

When evaluating an intervention programme (e.g. a new teaching method) across multiple schools AND by gender, two-way ANOVA answers not only "does the programme work" (main effect) but also "does its effect differ between males and females" (interaction). In the classroom data example above, no interaction was detected (p=0.315) — good news: a school-improvement policy is likely to benefit both genders fairly evenly, without needing a gender-specific intervention design.
