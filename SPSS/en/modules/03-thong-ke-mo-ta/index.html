1 · Missing data

Three types — not all equally "harmless"

TypeFeatureRisk level
MCARCompletely random, no patternLow — safest
MARPatterned, but not tied directly to the value being examinedModerate — needs checking
MNARPattern tied to the true value itselfHigh — can bias conclusions

🗣️ In plain terms: MCAR is like "a few pages randomly dropped while photocopying"; MNAR is like "someone hiding their real income because it's low" — the very reason for hiding is the thing you want to measure.

Real example

Variable h2 is missing 3/240 cases (1.25%)

No clear pattern among these 3 missing cases (not concentrated in one school or one gender) → reasonable to treat as MCAR or mild MAR, not MNAR.

Because the proportion is so tiny (1.25%), the effect on the final result is negligible whether listwise or pairwise deletion is used.

Listwise vs Pairwise

Two ways to delete missing data, two different costs

Listwise: drops the whole case if ANY variable entered into the analysis is missing — even a variable unrelated to the question at hand.

Pairwise: only drops a case for the variable CURRENTLY being calculated — other calculations on the same file keep their own N.

Example: one student missing only variable h2 → listwise drops that student from EVERY analysis in that run, even ones that don't use h2 at all.

A third option

Imputation — deletion isn't the only choice

Instead of deleting, fill the missing cell with a plausible value (e.g. that variable's mean, or a value predicted from other variables).

The cost: it shrinks the data's TRUE variability (the computed SD becomes smaller than reality) and can distort the correlation between the imputed variable and others.

Rule of thumb: a very small missing rate (like h2's 1.25%) → simple deletion is simpler and safer; a large missing rate (>10%) is when imputation becomes worth considering.

Cross-field examples

Three types of missing data across three fields

FieldSituationType
Education (real, this site)3/240 students missing the h2 interest score, no clear patternMCAR
Healthcare (illustrative)Patients drop out of treatment because of SEVERE side effects — the severity itself is the reason for dropping outMNAR
Economics/surveys (illustrative)Low-income households tend to refuse to answer the income questionMNAR

Both the healthcare and economics examples are MNAR — the most dangerous kind, because the true value itself determines whether it's missing, which tends to bias the final conclusion toward looking "better than reality".

2 · Crosstabs

Trap: uneven group sizes make raw counts misleading

Always compare with ROW percentages when group sizes differ — overall-table percentages are easily dominated by the larger group.

Real example #1

Gender × study method (240 students)

ProjectTraditionalTotal
Male (n=121)63 (52.1%)58 (47.9%)121
Female (n=119)52 (43.7%)67 (56.3%)119

Row % for males: 63/121 = 0.521 → 52.1%. 63>52 (raw counts) is easy to misread — but row % differ by only ~8 points.

Real example #2

School × method — 3 schools, three different stories

SchoolProjectTraditional% choosing Project
A (n=80)443655.0%
B (n=99)465346.5%
C (n=61)253641.0%

School A has a much HIGHER Project-method rate than School C (55.0% vs 41.0%) despite a smaller sample (80 vs 61) — a different crosstab tells a different story; the conclusions cannot be pooled.

Cross-field examples

Crosstabs in two fields outside education

FieldRow × column variableTrap to avoid
Healthcare (illustrative)Gender × on-time follow-up visitThe female patient group is larger → higher raw count of follow-ups even if the follow-up RATE could be lower than males
Economics/region (illustrative)Region (urban/rural) × near-poor householdUrban areas are more populous → higher raw count of near-poor households even though the rural near-poor RATE is actually higher

The same lesson in all 3 fields: raw counts reflect group SIZE, while row % reflect the true TREND inside each group.

Choosing the right chart

Chart type must match the variable's scale

  • Bar chart (separated bars): for nominal/ordinal data (e.g. school A/B/C, gender) — bars are NOT adjacent because there is no continuous order.
  • Histogram: for continuous interval/ratio data (e.g. maths score) — bars are adjacent, showing a frequency distribution.
  • Boxplot: shows BOTH central tendency and dispersal at once, and compares several groups side by side (e.g. maths score across 3 schools).
  • Scatterplot: NOT used in this module — reserved for examining the relationship between two continuous variables (Module 08).

3 · Central tendency

Mode · Median · Mean — when to use which?

MeasureScaleRobust to outliers?
ModeAny scale, especially nominalYes
MedianOrdinal+Yes
MeanInterval, ratioNo

🗣️ In plain terms: the Mode is "what most people pick", the Median is "the person standing right in the middle of the line", the Mean is "split evenly among everyone" — the mean is easily pulled by one extreme case, the other two are not.

Mean formula

Sample mean — worked step by step

Sample mean
x̄ = Σxᵢ / n
  • x̄sample mean
  • Σxᵢsum of all observed values
  • nnumber of observations in the sample

Example, 3 maths scores {6, 7, 8}: x̄ = (6+7+8)/3 = 21/3 = 7.

Dispersal formula

Variance & standard deviation — worked step by step

Sample variance & standard deviation
s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)   ·   s = √s²
  • (xᵢ − x̄)each observation's deviation from the mean
  • n − 1degrees of freedom — dividing by n−1 gives an unbiased estimate (Bessel's correction)
  • sstandard deviation — same unit as the raw data, easier to interpret than s²

Continuing {6,7,8}: deviations −1,0,1 → squared 1,0,1 → sum=2 → s²=2/(3−1)=1 → s=√1=1.

Dispersal — real example

Three sets with the SAME mean=6, very different SDs

SetValuesSD
1 (has an outlier)1,2,3,4,207.91
24,5,6,6,91.87
3 (tightly clustered)5,6,6,6,70.71

7.91 / 0.71 ≈ 11.2× — the outlier set's SD is more than 11 times the tightly-clustered set's, despite the SAME mean=6.

SD · Range · IQR

Three dispersal measures, three levels of outlier robustness

SD: the average distance of each point from the mean — uses EVERY data point.

Range: max−min — uses only 2 extreme points, very sensitive to outliers.

IQR: Q3−Q1 — only looks at the middle 50% of the data, far more robust than the range.

Real example — pretest (n=240): Q1=52.18, Q3=68.12 → IQR = 68.12−52.18 = 15.94.

Cross-field examples

Interpreting SD across three fields

FieldVariable measuredWhat a large SD means
Education (real)Maths score (SD=9.68/100)Students have fairly even ability around the mean of 47.9
Healthcare (illustrative)Systolic blood pressure in one patient groupA large SD → patients differ a great deal, making a single shared treatment protocol harder to apply evenly
Economics (illustrative)Household income in one regionA large SD → a clear rich-poor gap within the same region

4 · Skewness

Skewness — which side does the long tail point to?

🗣️ In plain terms: picture a bell curve pulled sideways — whichever direction the long tail stretches toward, that's the sign of the skew.

Skewness signShapeMean vs Median
Positive (+)Right-skewed, long tail toward LARGE valuesMean > Median
≈ 0Roughly symmetricMean ≈ Median
Negative (−)Left-skewed, long tail toward SMALL valuesMean < Median

Real example: pretest has mean=60.35 < median=60.95 → matching a measured skewness of −0.097 (a very slight negative skew).

Real example — an outlier

study_hours: skewness=7.42 — a sign of a data-entry error

One student has study_hours=48 (48 hours a day — impossible, a day only has 24 hours) pulling the skewness from a normal range up to 7.42 — extreme compared to the other variables (pretest −0.10, posttest −0.15, math +0.15).

Lesson: a skewness value far larger than that of comparable variables is the FIRST signal to suspect a data-entry error, before trusting any analysis that uses that variable.

Kurtosis

Kurtosis — is the peak sharper or flatter than normal?

Kurtosis signNameShape
≈ 0MesokurticPeak similar to a normal distribution
Positive (+)LeptokurticSharper peak, heavier tails (more outliers)
Negative (−)PlatykurticFlatter peak, thinner tails

Same study_hours example: kurtosis=86.93 (extreme, caused by the same 48-hour-a-day outlier above) — compare to pretest −0.54, posttest −0.19, math −0.33, all close to normal.

Cross-field examples

Skewness commonly seen in two fields outside education

FieldVariableTypical shape
Economics (illustrative)Personal income in a countryVery strongly right-skewed — most incomes are low/moderate, a small number of very wealthy individuals stretch a long tail

Both examples are famously right-skewed in real life — exactly why income reports use the "median", not the "mean" (the mean is easily pulled by a handful of very rich/very sick cases).

5 · Taking stock

Matching statistics to the scale of data

All 4 scales (Module 01) → frequencies/%/mode/crosstabs can all be computed.

Ordinal and above → the median can also be added.

Only Interval & Ratio ("Scale" in SPSS) → only then can mean, SD, skewness, kurtosis be added.

Practical workflow

3 steps before describing ANY variable

  • Step 1 — Check for missing data: MCAR/MAR/MNAR, decide whether to delete or keep (Part 1).
  • Step 2 — Look at the shape before trusting the numbers: plot a boxplot/histogram, read the skewness/kurtosis (Part 4) — catching outliers/data-entry errors BEFORE computing a mean.
  • Step 3 — Choose the right central tendency/dispersal measure for the scale of data (Part 3), then report.
  • Skipping Step 2 is the most common mistake — many people compute the mean/SD right away without looking at the distribution, easily fooled by an outlier like study_hours=48.

1. Missing data — don't skip this before describing anything

Three types of missing data (Rubin, 1976 — cited in Cohen et al., p.753):

TypeDefinitionExample
MCAR — missing completely at randomNo pattern in the missing dataA scanner randomly fails to read some sheets
MAR — missing at randomThere is a pattern, but not tied directly to the main dependent variableMen more often skip an "emotion" item — related to gender, not directly to the score itself
MNAR — missing not at randomThe pattern of missingness is tied to the true value itselfLow-income households skip the income question

Two main approaches: deletion — listwise (drop a case if any variable is missing) or pairwise (drop only for the specific calculation) — and imputation — filling in a plausible value (e.g. the mean), though this reduces the data's true variability and can distort correlational analysis (p.754).

Example from the classroom data (240 students): the variable h2 has 3 missing values (1.25%). Since the proportion is tiny and no clear pattern is visible, pairwise deletion for correlations involving h2 is more sensible than dropping all 3 students from every analysis.

2. Frequencies, percentages & crosstabulations

SPSS/PSPP compute frequencies and percentages automatically (FREQUENCIES). Bar/pie charts suit nominal-ordinal data; histograms suit interval-ratio data; boxplots show central tendency and dispersal at once (pp.754–757).

Crosstab trap: uneven group sizes make raw counts misleading

Gender × study method crosstab (240 students):

ProjectTraditionalTotal
Male (n=121)63 (52.1%)58 (47.9%)121
Female (n=119)52 (43.7%)67 (56.3%)119

Looking at raw counts, 63 > 52 suggests "males clearly chose the project method more than females". But row percentages show 52.1% of males vs 43.7% of females — a real but smaller gap than it first appears. This is exactly the trap Cohen et al. warn about with their public-library example (Tables 40.3–40.4, p.758): always report both counts and percentages, and prefer row percentages when group sizes are uneven.

A second real example — School × method (3 schools): School A chooses the project method 55.0% of the time (44/80), School B 46.5% (46/99), School C only 41.0% (25/61). School A and School C differ by 14 percentage points despite both being real subsets of the same 240-student sample — a reminder that a single overall figure can hide very different stories at the school level.

3. Central tendency & dispersal

MeasureSuitable scaleRobust to outliers?
ModeAny scale, especially nominalYes
MedianOrdinal and aboveYes
MeanInterval, ratioNo — easily pulled by outliers

Three measures of dispersal: standard deviation (SD) — average distance of each score from the mean; range — max−min, very sensitive to outliers; IQR — Q3−Q1, more robust than the range.

Sample mean
x̄ = Σxᵢ / n
  • x̄sample mean
  • Σxᵢsum of all observed values
  • nnumber of observations in the sample
Sample variance & standard deviation
s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)   ·   s = √s²
  • s²sample variance — the average squared deviation from x̄
  • (xᵢ − x̄)each observation's deviation from the mean
  • n − 1degrees of freedom — dividing by n−1 (not n) gives an unbiased estimate of the population variance (Bessel's correction)
  • ssample standard deviation — the square root of the variance, same unit as the raw data so easier to interpret than s²
Worked by hand on a small example: 3 maths scores {6, 7, 8} → x̄ = (6+7+8)/3 = 7. Deviations: (6−7)=−1, (7−7)=0, (8−7)=1. Squared: 1, 0, 1 → sum=2. s² = 2/(3−1) = 1 → s = √1 = 1. Interpretation: scores vary on average by about ±1 around the mean of 7.
Lesson: never report the mean alone. Set (1), with an outlier (the value 20), has an SD of 7.91 — 11 times set (3)'s, despite an identical mean of 6. Always report the SD or plot the distribution.

3bis. Distribution shape — skewness & kurtosis

Beyond central tendency and dispersal, a third dimension describes a distribution: its SHAPE. Skewness describes which side the long tail leans toward; kurtosis describes whether the peak is sharper or flatter than a normal distribution.

Skewness signShapeMean vs Median
Positive (+)Right-skewed, long tail toward large valuesMean > Median
≈ 0Roughly symmetricMean ≈ Median
Negative (−)Left-skewed, long tail toward small valuesMean < Median
Kurtosis signNameShape
≈ 0MesokurticPeak similar to a normal distribution
Positive (+)LeptokurticSharper peak, heavier tails (more outliers)
Negative (−)PlatykurticFlatter peak, thinner tails
Real example — an outlier makes skewness/kurtosis explode: the variable study_hours has skewness=7.424 and kurtosis=86.930 — wildly extreme compared to pretest (−0.097, −0.535), posttest (−0.147, −0.193) and math (+0.148, −0.333). The cause: a single student recorded 48 study hours in one day, which is physically impossible (a day only has 24 hours). A skewness/kurtosis value far outside the range of comparable variables is the first signal to suspect a data-entry error, before trusting any analysis built on that variable.

Income (a country's personal income distribution) and hospital length-of-stay after surgery are both famously right-skewed in real life (illustrative examples, not this site's dataset) — exactly why income reports prefer the "median" over the "mean".

4. Taking stock — matching statistics to the scale of data

Summary rule (p.765): all four scales support frequencies/percentages/mode/crosstabs. Ordinal and above supports the median. Only interval and ratio data (merged as "Scale" in SPSS) support the mean, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis.

A 3-step workflow before describing any variable: (1) check for missing data — MCAR/MAR/MNAR, decide whether to delete or keep; (2) look at the shape before trusting the numbers — plot a boxplot/histogram, read skewness/kurtosis, catching outliers or data-entry errors before computing a mean; (3) choose the right central tendency/dispersal measure for the variable's scale, then report. Skipping step 2 is the most common mistake — many people jump straight to the mean/SD without looking at the distribution shape, and get fooled by an outlier like study_hours=48.

A. Descriptive statistics for maths score (Frequencies):

  1. Open the data file: File → Open → Data... → select lop_hoc_240.sav → Open.
  2. Go to Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Frequencies...
  3. In the dialog: click to select math in the left-hand list, then click the ▶ arrow in the middle to send it to the Variable(s) box on the right.
  4. Click the Statistics... button at the bottom of the dialog.
  5. In the new window: check Mean, Median, Std. deviation, Minimum, Maximum, Skewness → click Continue.
  6. Click OK in the main dialog.
  7. Results appear in the Output Viewer: find the "Statistics" table and read the Mean/Median/Std. Deviation rows for the math column.

B. Boxplot of maths score by school (Explore):

  1. Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Explore...
  2. Send math to the Dependent List box (select the variable, then click the arrow next to that box).
  3. Send school to the Factor List box.
  4. Click Plots..., under "Boxplots" select Factor levels together → Continue.
  5. Click OK → the boxplot appears in the Output Viewer, comparing the 3 school boxes visually.

C. Crosstabulation of gender × teaching method (Crosstabs):

  1. Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs...
  2. Send gender to the Row(s) box, send method to the Column(s) box.
  3. Click Cells..., under "Percentages" check Row → Continue.
  4. Click OK → read the frequency + row-percentage table in the Output.

📜 Theoretical & Historical Background

Descriptive statistics isn't a single invention but a convergence of several historical threads. Before the 19th century, "statistics" was nearly synonymous with counting a population for the state (the word "statistics" itself comes from "state"). The major turning point came when social reformers began using numbers to PERSUADE, not just to count.

A classic example: Florence Nightingale (1820–1910) — the founder of modern nursing — during the Crimean War collected mortality data on soldiers and discovered that most deaths came from infectious disease caused by poor sanitation, not combat. She devised a fan-shaped chart (the "coxcomb"/"rose diagram") to present this data to the British Parliament — one of the earliest and most famous examples of DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS genuinely changing public policy.

Technically, many tools you learn in this module (boxplots, "whiskers", exploratory data analysis) come from statistician John Tukey, who published Exploratory Data Analysis in 1977. Before Tukey, statistics focused mainly on hypothesis testing (Modules 05–07); Tukey emphasised LOOKING at data first — exactly the "plot first, trust numbers second" spirit running through this module. The concepts of skewness and kurtosis themselves trace back to Karl Pearson's work in the 1890s, part of the same programme that gave statistics the modern "standard deviation".

Timeline relevant to this module
  • Mid-19th century — Florence Nightingale uses fan-shaped charts to present military mortality data, persuading the British government to reform hospital sanitation.
  • 1893 — Karl Pearson introduces the term "standard deviation" in its modern sense, and formalises skewness and kurtosis as measures of distribution shape.
  • 1977 — John Tukey publishes Exploratory Data Analysis, popularising the boxplot and the mindset of "exploring data visually before testing it".

🔎 Real-World Case Studies

Case 1 — Nightingale and the chart that changed a war

During the Crimean War, the British army lost more soldiers to disease than to combat. The raw figures (monthly mortality tables by cause) already existed, but no one acted — until Nightingale re-presented them visually. This is this module's "40.2 Frequencies & charts" lesson at historical scale: the same data, presented differently, determines whether readers act.

Case 2 — Tukey and "look before you trust"

Before the 1970s, many researchers ran hypothesis tests (t-tests, ANOVA) directly without examining the data's distribution. Tukey showed that a dataset can "pass" a test while still containing data-entry errors, outliers, or an odd distribution that only a boxplot/histogram would reveal. The three same-mean sets in the slide above are a miniature version of Tukey's lesson: NEVER trust a single summary number without looking at the shape of the data. This module's own study_hours=48 outlier is a live demonstration of exactly this lesson.
