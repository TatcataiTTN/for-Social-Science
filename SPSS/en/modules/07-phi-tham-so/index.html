1. The Chi-square (χ²) test

Chi-square is the most common test for nominal data, comparing OBSERVED frequencies with EXPECTED frequencies in a crosstabulation. Expected per cell = (row total × column total) / grand total (pp.789–790).

Cohen et al.'s example (pp.789–790): 195 people (male/female) voting like/dislike maths. Chi-square yields χ²=28.87, compared to the critical value 9.21 (0.01 level) → 28.87>9.21, highly statistically significant. Write-up: "A statistically significant difference was found between the males and females (χ²=28.87, df=2, p=0.000)."

The 20% rule — chi-square's key limitation (p.791): no more than 20% of cells in the table should have fewer than 5 observations. The book's Table 41.15 example (a 2×5 table) has 30% of cells <5 → results are UNRELIABLE even though the computer still returns a number. If >25% of cells are small, use Fisher's Exact Probability Test (SPSS computes this automatically when needed).

Yates's correction (continuity correction): compensates for chi-square being over-estimated in 2×2 tables; SPSS applies this automatically for 2×2 tables.

Effect size: 2×2 tables use the phi coefficient (thresholds like Cohen's d: small=0.10, medium=0.30, large=0.50); larger tables use Cramer's V (pp.791–792).

Comparing two real chi-square results on the classroom data:
  • Gender × Teaching method: χ²(1)=1.37, p=0.243 → not significant.
  • Gender × School (2×3 table): χ²(2)=0.14, p=0.932, 0% of cells violate the 20% rule (reliable result) → not significant, Cramer's V≈0.024 (near zero).
Both are valid 'not found' examples — unlike the book's highly significant example, showing chi-square doesn't always yield significant results with real data.

2. Degrees of Freedom

The intuition (per Cohen & Holliday, quoted pp.792–793): choosing 5 numbers with a fixed total=25 → the first 4 are FREE to choose, the 5th is CONSTRAINED → df=4=N−1. Each additional constraint reduces degrees of freedom by 1.

For a crosstabulation: df = (rows − 1) + (columns − 1). The book's 2×3 example (p.792): (2−1)+(3−1)=3.

3. Mann-Whitney U test

The non-parametric equivalent of the independent-samples t-test — used for 2 independent samples with at least 1 ordinal variable, no normal-distribution assumption needed. Based on RANKS: counting how many times a score from one group ranks higher than one from the other (p.793).

The book's example (pp.793–794): a course evaluation by gender (5-point scale), U=2,732.500, p=0.019 → significant, males felt more strongly than females that the course allowed learning at their own pace.

Key limitation: unlike a t-test (reading means directly) or Tukey (homogeneous subsets table), Mann-Whitney only tells you WHETHER a difference exists — finding exactly where requires returning to the frequency crosstabulation (p.794).

4. Wilcoxon test

The non-parametric equivalent of the paired-samples t-test — used when the SAME group provides two values (two variables/time points) but data don't meet parametric conditions (pp.795–796).

The book's example: the same group rating two course variables ("the course was just right" and "the lecturer was well prepared"), a significant result (p=0.000) — the group rated "the lecturer was well prepared" more positively.

Real classroom data: Wilcoxon comparing two rating items a1 and a2 (same 240 students answered both): p=0.920 — completely NO difference (mean a1=2.908, a2=2.904, very close).

5. Kruskal-Wallis test

The non-parametric equivalent of one-way ANOVA — used for 3+ independent groups with ordinal data (p.797).

The book's example: 4 teacher groups by years of experience rating a course aspect, χ²=11.595, p=0.009 → significant. Like Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis does NOT automatically indicate which group differs — one must return to the crosstabulation: the 16-18-year group was most positive (p.798).

Real classroom data: Kruskal-Wallis on h1 ratings by 3 schools: H=2.844, p=0.241 — not significant, unlike the significant result (F=5.87, p=0.003) when comparing maths scores by school in Module 06 (different variables, different answers).

6. Friedman test

The non-parametric equivalent of repeated-measures ANOVA — used when the SAME group rates 3+ variables/time points, ordinal data (pp.798–799).

The book's example: the same group rating 3 course variables, χ²=0.353, p=0.838 → NOT significant, H0 supported.

Real classroom data: Friedman on 3 items a1, a2, a3 (same 240 students): χ²=0.555, p=0.758 — the same pattern as the book's example: the group rates related aspects quite evenly.
A key commonality among the 4 non-parametric tests: Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman all give ONLY statistical significance, NOT effect size — unlike t-test/ANOVA (Cohen's d, eta²). Friedman does have Kendall's W, but it's typically used to measure rater agreement rather than effect size between variables (p.799).

📜 Theoretical & Historical Background

The chi-square test was introduced by Karl Pearson in 1900, one of the earliest inferential statistical tools, predating even the t-test (1908) and ANOVA (1920s). Rank-based non-parametric tests developed much later: Frank Wilcoxon introduced his eponymous test in 1945; Henry Mann and Donald Whitney extended it into the Mann-Whitney U test in 1947; William Kruskal and W. Allen Wallis introduced Kruskal-Wallis in 1952; Milton Friedman (the Nobel-laureate economist, NOT a professional statistician) introduced the Friedman test in 1937 as a graduate student, before even Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney.

Timeline
  • 1900 — Karl Pearson introduces the chi-square test.
  • 1937 — Milton Friedman introduces his eponymous test (before Wilcoxon/Mann-Whitney!).
  • 1945 — Frank Wilcoxon introduces the signed-rank test.
  • 1947 — Henry Mann & Donald Whitney extend it into the Mann-Whitney U test.
  • 1952 — William Kruskal & W. Allen Wallis introduce the Kruskal-Wallis test.

🔎 Real Case Study

Case 1 — Why chi-square's "flexibility" makes it easy to misuse

Because chi-square only needs nominal data (easily collected via survey), it's used very widely — but the 20% rule (no more than 20% of cells with <5 observations) is often overlooked in practice, especially with small-sample surveys split into many small categories (like the book's 5-subject × 2-gender example, p.791). Lesson: a crosstabulation that "looks fine" with a large total sample (191 people) can still violate the rule if the data are unevenly distributed.

Case 2 — When to choose non-parametric tests instead of "forcing" a t-test/ANOVA

In attitude surveys using a 5-point Likert scale (very common in educational/psychological research), many novice researchers are still accustomed to "forcing" a t-test/ANOVA on this data even though it's inherently ordinal, not necessarily equal-interval between levels. Mann-Whitney/Kruskal-Wallis are the safer choice assumption-wise — though at the cost of directly computing effect size like t-test/ANOVA can, a trade-off between the CORRECTNESS of assumptions and the DETAIL of information obtained.
