1. Four safety checks before running a t-test

A t-test checks whether there is a statistically significant difference between the means of TWO groups/conditions. Cohen et al. (p.777) list 4 conditions that must be met to trust the result:

#ConditionIf violated
1Continuous data, dependent variable at interval/ratio levelSwitch to a non-parametric test (e.g. Mann-Whitney U)
2Random samplingHarder to generalise to the population
3Normal distributionLarge samples (n>30/group) usually compensate (Central Limit Theorem)
4Equality of variance between the two groupsRead the "not assumed" Levene row instead of "assumed"
Verified on real data: Shapiro-Wilk on gain for both teaching-method groups: Project p=0.888, Traditional p=0.587 (both >0.05) → the normality condition is well satisfied in this example.

2. Independent-samples t-test

Used when two groups are COMPLETELY unrelated (e.g. males and females, Project and Traditional). The null hypothesis (H0) always states "no" difference between the two group means (p.777).

Key to reading the output — Levene's Test: SPSS/PSPP always prints two t-test result rows: "Equal variances assumed" and "Equal variances not assumed". Levene's test (its own H0: "the two variances are equal") decides which row to read:
  • Levene p<0.05 → variances NOT equal → read the second row ("not assumed", using the Welch t).
  • Levene p≥0.05 → variances equal → read the first row ("assumed").

Cohen et al.'s example (pp.777–778): comparing leaders' and teachers' perceptions (0-10 scale) of "leadership commitment". Levene p=0.004 (<0.05) → read the "not assumed" row → Sig.(2-tailed)=0.044 → statistically significant. If the "assumed" row is (wrongly) read, Sig.=0.055 → a WRONG conclusion of "not significant"! Their second example (p.779), on "attention to teaching and learning": Levene p=0.728 (≥0.05) → read the "assumed" row → Sig.=0.610 → not statistically significant. Cohen et al. stress this is a "win-win" result: a shared perception (even a negative one) between the two groups is also a valuable finding, opening prospects for collaborative improvement.

Correct APA-style write-up (per the p.778 template): "The mean score gain of the Project group (M=8.66, SD=5.77) was statistically significantly higher (t(238)=7.59, p<0.001) than the Traditional group (M=2.89, SD=6.00)." Do not infer causation from this figure alone.

Contrast with a NON-significant case (from the same classroom data): comparing maths scores between Males (n=121, M=47.08, SD=10.33) and Females (n=119, M=48.76, SD=8.92). Levene p=0.088 (≥0.05, but fairly close to the threshold — warranting caution) → read the "assumed" row: t=-1.348, df=238, p=0.179. Conclusion: insufficient evidence of a gender difference in maths scores in this sample — a "not found" result also needs to be reported honestly, without "p-hacking" until significance appears.

3. Paired-samples t-test

Used when the SAME group of subjects provides two values: two time points (before/after), two variables, or two conditions. Cohen et al.'s example (pp.779–780): the same 1,000 people rating two different aspects ("attention to teaching and learning" and "quality of lesson preparation").

Why is the paired t-test more powerful than the independent one here? Pretest and posttest of the SAME student correlate very highly (r=0.857, see Module 04/08). Pairing removes the portion of variation due to INDIVIDUAL differences between students (a strong student tends to score high on both), leaving only variation due to the EFFECT of instruction — making the test much more powerful than an independent t-test with the same sample size.
Common mistake: if two columns of data actually measure the SAME subjects (before/after, two variables on the same people) but an INDEPENDENT t-test is run instead, the result ignores the correlation between the two measurements, making the test much less powerful and potentially leading to a wrong conclusion (missing a genuinely significant difference).

4. Beyond statistical significance — always report effect size

As Module 04 emphasised: statistical significance (the p-value) only tells you "is this due to chance", not "how big is the difference". For the Project vs Traditional example above: Cohen's d = 0.981 (moderate-to-large, close to the "large" 1.00 threshold). Combining t=7.59 (highly significant) AND d=0.981 (a substantial effect) gives the fullest picture — both metrics should be reported, not just one.

📜 Theoretical & Historical Background

The t-test is called "Student's t-test" thanks to a rather interesting story: statistician William Sealy Gosset worked for the Guinness brewery in Dublin from 1899, in charge of quality-controlling raw materials (barley) using small samples. Guinness forbade employees from publishing research under their real name (fearing trade-secret leaks), so in 1908 Gosset published the t distribution in Biometrika under the pseudonym "Student". The t distribution solved his exact practical problem: for SMALL sample sizes (like his trial barley batches), the normal distribution was no longer a good approximation for the distribution of the sample mean — a "fatter-tailed" distribution was needed to account for the uncertainty of estimating the standard deviation from a small sample. Ronald Fisher later extended the theory and popularised the t-test widely during the 1920s.

Timeline
  • 1899 — Gosset joins the Guinness brewery, Dublin.
  • 1908 — Gosset publishes the t distribution in Biometrika under the pseudonym "Student".
  • 1920s — Ronald Fisher extends the theory and popularises the t-test in scientific research.
  • 1960s onward — The t-test becomes a standard test in SPSS and other statistical software.

🔎 Real Case Study

Case 1 — Misreading the Levene row can reverse an entire study

Cohen et al.'s real example above (pp.777–778) is not merely theoretical: Sig.(2-tailed)=0.044 (correct) vs. 0.055 (if the wrong row is read) — a tiny numerical difference, but enough to flip "significant" into "not significant" at the conventional 0.05 threshold. This is a common real-world mistake for SPSS beginners: looking only at the Sig.(2-tailed) column while ignoring the Levene column to its left.

Case 2 — Clinical trials: why "before-after" designs always favour a paired t-test

In medical/educational experimental research, the most common design measures the SAME group of subjects before and after an intervention (as in the pretest/posttest example above). If this data is accidentally treated as two independent groups (discarding the "these are the same people" information), the study loses much of the statistical power the paired design provides — potentially causing a GENUINELY effective intervention to be wrongly concluded "not significant" simply because the wrong test type was chosen.
